Topic 1: Numbers and Algebra

1.1 Scientific notation

  • Operations with numbers in the form a \times 10^k, where 1 \le a < 10 and k is an integer.
  • Calculator notation is not accepted as final scientific notation.

1.2 Arithmetic sequences and series

nth term

u_n = u_1 + (n-1)d

Sum of first n terms

S_n = \frac{n}{2}(2u_1 + (n-1)d)

or

S_n = \frac{n}{2}(u_1 + u_n)

Sigma notation

\sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i

1.3 Geometric sequences and series

nth term

u_n = u_1 r^{n-1}

Sum of first n terms

S_n = \frac{u_1(1-r^n)}{1-r}, \quad r \ne 1

1.4 Financial applications of geometric sequences and series

Compound interest

FV = PV\left(1+\frac{r}{100k}\right)^{kn}

Where:

  • PV = present value
  • FV = future value
  • r = annual interest rate (%)
  • k = compounding periods per year
  • n = number of years
  • Includes annual depreciation and other geometric financial models.

1.5 Laws of exponents with integer exponents and introduction to logarithms

Integer exponent laws

a^m a^n = a^{m+n}
\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}
(a^m)^n=a^{mn}
(ab)^n=a^n b^n

Definition

a^x = b \iff x = \log_a b \quad (a>0,\ a\ne1)

Natural logarithm

\ln x = \log_e x
  • Includes base 10 and base e logarithms.
  • Includes numerical evaluation of logarithms using technology.

1.6 Simple deductive proof

  • Numerical and algebraic proof.
  • LHS-to-RHS proof structure.
  • Equality versus identity.

1.7 Laws of exponents and logarithms

Rational exponent laws

a^{\frac{1}{m}}=\sqrt[m]{a}
a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}

Logarithm laws

\log_a(xy)=\log_a x+\log_a y
\log_a\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)=\log_a x-\log_a y
\log_a(x^m)=m\log_a x

Change of base

\log_a x=\frac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}
  • Includes solving exponential equations using logarithms.

1.8 Sum of infinite geometric sequences

Sum to infinity

S_\infty = \frac{u_1}{1-r}, \quad |r|<1

1.9 Binomial theorem

For n \in \mathbb{N}:

(a+b)^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}a^{n-r}b^r

Binomial coefficient

\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}

Additional higher level

  • 1.10 Counting principles and the binomial theorem for rational indices
    Counting principles (multiplication and addition rules), permutations and combinations of distinct objects, with arrangements of identical objects and circular arrangements excluded. Extends the binomial theorem to (a + b)^n for rational n, written as a^n(1 + b/a)^n, as an infinite series valid for |b/a| < 1 and used for approximations; proof of the theorem is not required.
  • 1.11 Partial fractions
    Split a proper rational expression into partial fractions when the denominator has at most two distinct linear factors (degree of numerator less than that of the denominator). Repeated factors, irreducible quadratic factors and improper fractions are outside this statement; the main use is rearranging integrands in 5.15.
  • 1.12 Complex numbers in Cartesian form and the complex plane
    The imaginary unit i with i² = −1, complex numbers in Cartesian form a + bi, and the vocabulary of real part, imaginary part, conjugate, modulus and argument. Includes arithmetic in Cartesian form and representing complex numbers as points or vectors in the complex plane (Argand diagram).
  • 1.13 Modulus-argument and Euler forms of complex numbers
    Write complex numbers in modulus-argument form r(cos θ + i sin θ) = r cis θ and Euler form re^(iθ), and convert freely between these and Cartesian form. Carry out sums, products and quotients in any form and interpret them geometrically (multiplying moduli and adding arguments as an enlargement and rotation).
  • 1.14 Conjugate roots, De Moivre's theorem, powers and roots of complex numbers
    Non-real roots of polynomial equations with real coefficients come in conjugate pairs. De Moivre's theorem (r cis θ)^n = r^n cis nθ, proved by induction for positive integers n and known to hold more widely, is used for powers, nth roots of complex numbers (including roots of unity) and rational exponents.
  • 1.15 Proof by induction, by contradiction and by counterexample
    Proof by mathematical induction (for example sums of series, divisibility, derivatives and complex numbers), proof by contradiction (for example irrationality results and the infinitude of primes), and disproof by a counterexample, where the student must show why the example breaks the statement rather than just stating it.
  • 1.16 Systems of linear equations in up to three unknowns
    Solve systems of up to three linear equations in up to three unknowns, both by algebra (elimination or row reduction) and by technology. Decide whether a system has a unique solution, infinitely many (giving the general solution in terms of a parameter) or none (inconsistent); links to intersecting planes in 3.18.

Topic 1: Numbers and Algebra

  • 1.1 Scientific notation
    Operations with numbers in the form a x 10^k, 1 <= a < 10, k integer.
  • 1.2 Arithmetic sequences and series
    nth term, sum of n terms, sigma notation, applications, modelling with arithmetic sequences.
  • 1.3 Geometric sequences and series
    nth term, sum of n terms of a geometric sequence, applications (growth/decay, population).
  • 1.4 Financial applications of geometric sequences and series
    Compound interest, annual depreciation, TVM solver use.
  • 1.5 Laws of exponents with integer exponents and introduction to logarithms
    Integer exponent laws; a^x = b <=> x = log_a b; base 10 and e; numerical evaluation of logs with technology.
  • 1.6 Approximation, bounds, percentage error and estimation
    Round to decimal places and significant figures, choosing a sensible accuracy for the data and context (for example currency). Find upper and lower bounds of rounded values, calculate percentage errors including the maximum percentage error caused by measurement rounding, and judge whether a calculated result is reasonable.
  • 1.7 Amortization and annuities using technology
    Use technology (the GDC finance solver or a spreadsheet) to solve loan amortization and annuity problems: repayments, number of payments, interest rate, outstanding balance and total interest. In examinations payments are at the end of each period; the annuity formula helps understanding but is not examined.
  • 1.8 Solving linear systems and polynomial equations with technology
    Use technology to solve systems of linear equations in up to three unknowns and polynomial equations, using the terms zeros and roots. No particular method is required, and exam systems always have a unique solution.

Additional higher level

  • 1.9 Laws of logarithms
    Use the laws of logarithms for products, quotients and powers, with base 10 or e in examinations and positive arguments. Apply them to combine or expand expressions and to solve equations.
  • 1.10 Simplifying expressions with rational exponents
    Simplify numerical and algebraic expressions with rational (fractional) exponents, using the laws of exponents and the link between fractional powers and roots, including negative fractional exponents.
  • 1.11 Sum of infinite geometric sequences
    S_inf = u_1/(1-r) for |r| < 1; condition for convergence.
  • 1.12 Complex numbers in Cartesian form
    Introduce i with i² = −1 and complex numbers z = a + bi, with real and imaginary parts, conjugate, modulus and argument. Add, subtract, multiply and divide by hand and with technology, find powers with technology, draw Argand diagrams, and solve real quadratics with negative discriminant, linking to the graph not meeting the x-axis.
  • 1.13 Polar and exponential forms of complex numbers
    Write complex numbers in polar form r(cos θ + i sin θ) = r cis θ and exponential form re^(iθ), and convert between these and Cartesian form by hand and with technology. Multiply, divide and raise to integer powers in polar or exponential form (finding roots is not required), add sinusoids of equal frequency using complex numbers, and interpret addition as vector addition and multiplication as a rotation with a stretch.
  • 1.14 Matrices: algebra, determinants, inverses and linear systems
    Use matrix terms (element, row, column, order) and the algebra of matrices: equality, addition, subtraction, scalar and matrix multiplication, with the properties of multiplication and the identity and zero matrices. Find determinants and inverses by hand for 2 × 2 and with technology for larger matrices, and solve Ax = b with the inverse in contexts such as coding messages; A is always invertible in examinations.
  • 1.15 Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and diagonalisation of 2 × 2 matrices
    Find the characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues and eigenvectors of 2 × 2 matrices by hand and with technology. Diagonalise 2 × 2 matrices with distinct real eigenvalues and use Mⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹ to find powers in applications such as population movement between two places or predator–prey models.
