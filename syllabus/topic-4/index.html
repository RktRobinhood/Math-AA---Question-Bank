Topic 4: Statistics and Probability

4.1 Concepts, reliability and sampling techniques

  • Population, sample and random sample.
  • Discrete and continuous data.
  • Reliability and sampling methods.

4.2 Histograms, cumulative frequency graphs and box plots

  • Frequency distributions.
  • Histograms.
  • Cumulative frequency graphs.
  • Median, quartiles, percentiles, range and interquartile range.
  • Box-and-whisker diagrams.

4.3 Mean, median, mode, grouped mean, standard deviation, quartiles and IQR

Grouped mean

\bar{x}=\frac{\sum f_ix_i}{\sum f_i}

Interquartile range

\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1
  • Measures of central tendency and dispersion.
  • Standard deviation and variance using technology.

4.4 Pearson's, scatter diagrams and equation of y on x

  • Linear correlation of bivariate data.
  • Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient.
  • Scatter diagrams and lines of best fit.
  • Regression line of y on x.
  • Interpreting correlation and limits of extrapolation.

4.5 Probability concepts and expected numbers

P(A)=\frac{n(A)}{n(U)}
P(A')=1-P(A)
  • Trial, outcome, sample space and event.
  • Expected number of occurrences.

4.6 Combined, mutually exclusive, conditional, independence and probability diagrams

  • Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, sample-space diagrams and tables of outcomes.
  • Combined events:
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)
  • Conditional probability:
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
  • Independent events:
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)

4.7 Discrete random variables

E(X)=\sum x_iP(X=x_i)
  • Probability distributions.
  • Expected value and fair games.

4.8 Binomial distribution

X\sim B(n,p)
E(X)=np
\text{Var}(X)=np(1-p)

4.9 Normal distribution and calculations

  • Normal distribution and curve.
  • Normal probability calculations.
  • Inverse normal calculations where mean and standard deviation are given.

4.10 Regression line of x on y

  • Equation of the regression line of x on y.
  • Prediction using the appropriate regression direction.
  • Awareness that regression predictions cannot always be reversed.

4.11 Formal conditional probability and independence

  • Formal use of conditional probability formulae.
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)
  • Testing for independence.

4.12 Standardization of normal variables

Standardized variable

z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
  • Use of z-values.
  • Inverse normal calculations where mean or standard deviation is unknown.

Additional higher level

  • 4.13 Bayes' theorem
    Using Bayes' theorem to reverse a conditional probability, with at most three events in the partition, for example in medical testing where a test result is used to update the chance of having a disease. Builds on conditional probability and independence from SL.
  • 4.14 Discrete and continuous random variables: pdfs, mean, variance, median and mode
    Variance of discrete random variables, and continuous random variables defined by probability density functions (including piecewise ones) that integrate to 1. Covers the mode and median of a continuous variable, E(X), E(X²), Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]² and standard deviation for both types, fair games, and the effect of the linear transformation aX + b.

Topic 4: Statistics and Probability

  • 4.1 Concepts, reliability and sampling techniques
    Population, sample, random sample, discrete and continuous data; reliability and bias; interpretation of outliers (more than 1.5 x IQR from the nearest quartile); sampling techniques.
  • 4.2 Histograms, cumulative frequency graphs and box plots
    Frequency tables; histograms; cumulative frequency graphs (median, quartiles, percentiles); box and whisker diagrams.
  • 4.3 Mean, median, mode, grouped mean, standard deviation, quartiles and IQR
    Mean, median, mode; estimate of mean from grouped data; modal class; range, IQR, standard deviation, variance; effect of constant changes.
  • 4.4 Pearson's, scatter diagrams and equation of y on x
    Pearson's r; scatter diagrams; line of best fit; regression line of y on x; prediction; interpreting gradient and intercept.
  • 4.5 Probability concepts and expected numbers
    Trial, outcome, sample space, event; P(A) = n(A)/n(U); complementary events; expected number of occurrences.
  • 4.6 Combined, mutually exclusive, conditional, independence and probability diagrams
    Venn, tree and sample-space diagrams, tables of outcomes; P(A u B); mutually exclusive events; conditional probability; independent events.
  • 4.7 Discrete random variables
    Discrete random variables and their distributions; expected value E(X); fair games.
  • 4.8 Binomial distribution
    Binomial distribution B(n, p); mean and variance.
  • 4.9 Normal distribution and calculations
    Normal distribution properties and diagrams; probability calculations; inverse normal with known mean and standard deviation.
  • 4.10 Spearman's rank correlation coefficient
    Spearman's rank correlation coefficient rs, found with technology, with tied data values given the average of their ranks; knowing that Pearson's r measures only linear association while rs measures any monotonic association, and that rs is less affected by outliers. Deriving either coefficient is not required.
  • 4.11 Hypothesis testing: χ² tests and the t-test
    Writing null and alternative hypotheses and reaching conclusions with significance levels and p-values; χ² tests for independence (contingency tables up to 4 × 4, degrees of freedom, critical values) and goodness of fit, using expected and observed frequencies; the pooled two-sample t-test for unpaired samples from normal populations, one- or two-tailed. Exam tests are upper-tail at 1%, 5% or 10%, the statistic and p-value come from technology, and expected frequencies exceed 5.

Additional higher level

  • 4.12 Designing data collection; reliability and validity
    Designing valid data collection, including surveys and questionnaires with structured, unbiased and precise questions; choosing relevant variables and data to analyse; grouping numerical data into categories for a χ² test so that expected frequencies exceed 5, and reducing the degrees of freedom for each parameter estimated from the data; the difference between reliability and validity, with test-retest and parallel-forms reliability, and content and criterion-related validity.
  • 4.13 Non-linear regression and the coefficient of determination
    Fitting least squares regression curves with technology: linear, quadratic, cubic, exponential, power and sine models; the sum of square residuals SSres as a measure of fit; the coefficient of determination R² as the proportion of variation in y explained by the model, with R² = r² for linear models. R² alone is not a reliable way to choose between models; the formula R² = 1 − SSres/SStot is not examined.
  • 4.14 Linear combinations of random variables and unbiased estimates
    Expectation and variance of a linear transformation aX + b; the expected value of any linear combination of random variables and the variance of a linear combination of independent random variables; x̄ as an unbiased estimate of μ and s²ₙ₋₁ = n/(n − 1) × s²ₙ as an unbiased estimate of σ². Proofs that these estimators are unbiased are not examined.
  • 4.15 Combining normal variables and the central limit theorem
    Any linear combination of independent normal variables is normal, so X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n) when X ~ N(μ, σ²); the central limit theorem says the sample mean is approximately normal for large samples from any distribution, with n > 30 taken as large enough in exams.
  • 4.16 Confidence intervals for a mean
    Confidence intervals for the mean of a normal population, using the normal distribution when σ is known and the t-distribution when σ is unknown, whatever the sample size, and interpreting the interval in context.
  • 4.17 The Poisson distribution
    The Poisson distribution as a model for independent events occurring at a uniform average rate, with mean and variance both equal to λ; the sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson; choosing between normal, binomial and Poisson models in context. Proofs of mean and variance are not required.
  • 4.18 Critical regions, tests for a mean, a proportion or a correlation, and type I and II errors
    Critical values and critical regions; tests for a population mean using z when σ is known and t when σ is unknown, with matched pairs treated as one sample of differences; one-tailed tests for a proportion (binomial) and a mean (Poisson); testing ρ = 0 with technology; Type I and Type II errors and their probabilities for normal (known variance), binomial and Poisson tests. Critical regions for t-tests are not required, and for discrete tests the critical region is the largest one with probability below the significance level.
  • 4.19 Transition matrices and Markov chains
    Markov chains: transition diagrams and transition matrices T with Tᵢⱼ the probability of moving from state j to state i, initial state matrices, and the state after n steps sₙ = Tⁿs₀; regular Markov chains; steady-state and long-term probabilities found by high powers of T or by solving linear equations, seen as the eigenvector for eigenvalue 1.
